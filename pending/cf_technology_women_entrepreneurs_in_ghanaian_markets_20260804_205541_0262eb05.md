# Women Entrepreneurs In Ghanaian Markets

The history of women entrepreneurs in Ghanaian markets dates back to the pre-colonial era, where women played a significant role in the country's economy. In traditional Ghanaian society, women were responsible for managing the household, farming, and trading, which laid the foundation for their future entrepreneurial endeavors. This overview will explore the origins and development of women entrepreneurs in Ghanaian markets, tracing key events, figures, and turning points that have shaped their journey.

**Pre-Colonial Era (Before 1844)**
In pre-colonial Ghana, women were actively involved in trade and commerce, particularly in the markets of the Akan states, such as the Asante and Fante kingdoms. They traded in goods like gold, salt, and cloth, and were known for their expertise in crafting and selling traditional textiles, such as kente cloth. Women like Yaa Asantewaa, the queen mother of the Asante kingdom, were renowned for their business acumen and leadership skills, which inspired future generations of women entrepreneurs.

**Colonial Era (1844-1957)**
During the colonial period, the British colonial administration introduced new trade policies and infrastructure, which affected the traditional trading practices of Ghanaian women. Many women adapted to these changes by shifting their focus to local markets, where they sold foodstuffs, handicrafts, and other essential goods. Despite the challenges, women like Ama Ata Aidoo, a Ghanaian author and entrepreneur, emerged as pioneers in the field of commerce, using their writing and business skills to promote Ghanaian culture and products.

**Post-Independence Era (1957-1980)**
After Ghana gained independence in 1957, the government implemented policies aimed at promoting economic development and empowering women. The First Republican Constitution of 1960, for example, guaranteed equal rights for women, including the right to own property and engage in trade. Women like Esther Ocloo, a Ghanaian entrepreneur and philanthropist, took advantage of these opportunities to establish successful businesses, such as the Nkran Oil Mills, which produced palm oil and other edible oils.

**Structural Adjustment Programs (1980-1990)**
In the 1980s, Ghana implemented Structural Adjustment Programs (SAPs) aimed at liberalizing the economy and promoting private sector growth. While these programs created new opportunities for entrepreneurs, they also led to increased competition and economic instability, which affected many women's businesses. However, women like Theresa Oppong-Beeko, a Ghanaian entrepreneur and politician, persevered and adapted to the changing economic landscape, using their skills and resilience to build successful businesses.

**Contemporary Era (1990-Present)**
In recent years, Ghana has experienced significant economic growth, driven in part by the expansion of the services sector and the growth of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Women have played a crucial role in this growth, with many establishing successful businesses in areas like textiles, food processing, and tourism. Organizations like the Ghanaian Association of Women Entrepreneurs (GAWE) and the National Board for Small Scale Industries (NBSSI) have provided support and training for women entrepreneurs, helping them to access markets, finance, and other resources.

**Key Figures and Turning Points**
Several key figures and events have shaped the history of women entrepreneurs in Ghanaian markets. Some notable examples include:

1. **Yaa Asantewaa**: The queen mother of the Asante kingdom, who led a rebellion against British colonial rule in 1900 and inspired future generations of women entrepreneurs.
2. **Esther Ocloo**: A Ghanaian entrepreneur and philanthropist, who established the Nkran Oil Mills and became one of the first women to receive a loan from the Ghanaian government to support her business.
3. **Theresa Oppong-Beeko**: A Ghanaian entrepreneur and politician, who served as the Minister for Women and Children's Affairs and worked to promote women's economic empowerment.
4. **The Ghanaian Association of Women Entrepreneurs (GAWE)**: Established in 1991, GAWE has provided training, support, and advocacy for women entrepreneurs, helping them to access markets, finance, and other resources.
5. **The National Board for Small Scale Industries (NBSSI)**: Established in 1985, NBSSI has provided support and training for small and medium-sized enterprises, including those owned by women.
