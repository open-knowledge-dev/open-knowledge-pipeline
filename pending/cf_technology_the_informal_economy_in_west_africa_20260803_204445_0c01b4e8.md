# The Informal Economy In West Africa

The informal economy in West Africa, particularly in Ghana and Nigeria, is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon that has been the subject of extensive scholarly research and debate. The informal economy refers to economic activities that are not registered, regulated, or monitored by the state, and are often characterized by a lack of formal contracts, social protection, and labor rights (ILO, 2018). In Ghana and Nigeria, the informal economy is a significant sector, accounting for a substantial proportion of the workforce and contributing to the countries' GDP.

Classification and frameworks:
The informal economy can be classified into different categories, including the informal sector, informal employment, and informal entrepreneurship (Chen, 2012). The informal sector refers to economic activities that are not registered or regulated, such as street vending, small-scale farming, and artisanal crafts. Informal employment, on the other hand, refers to jobs that are not protected by labor laws or social protection, such as domestic work, construction work, and market trading. Informal entrepreneurship refers to businesses that are not registered or regulated, such as small-scale manufacturing, transportation, and retail trade.

The informal economy in Ghana and Nigeria can be understood through various frameworks, including the dualist approach, the structuralist approach, and the institutionalist approach (Meagher, 2010). The dualist approach views the informal economy as a separate and distinct sector from the formal economy, with its own unique characteristics and dynamics. The structuralist approach sees the informal economy as a result of structural factors, such as poverty, inequality, and lack of access to education and job opportunities. The institutionalist approach emphasizes the role of institutions, such as laws, regulations, and social norms, in shaping the informal economy.

Traditional knowledge systems:
In Ghana and Nigeria, traditional knowledge systems play a significant role in the informal economy. For example, in Ghana, the traditional apprenticeship system, known as "akpeteshie," provides training and skills development for young people in various trades, such as carpentry, blacksmithing, and weaving (Arhin, 2001). Similarly, in Nigeria, the traditional "apprenticeship" system, known as "igba," provides training and skills development for young people in various trades, such as tailoring, hairstyling, and mechanics (Meagher, 2010).

Academic perspectives:
Academic perspectives on the informal economy in Ghana and Nigeria are diverse and contested. Some scholars view the informal economy as a necessary evil, providing livelihoods and income opportunities for people who are excluded from the formal economy (Chen, 2012). Others see the informal economy as a source of exploitation and poverty, with workers facing poor working conditions, low wages, and lack of social protection (Meagher, 2010).

Some scholars have also highlighted the importance of the informal economy in promoting economic growth and development in Ghana and Nigeria. For example, a study by the International Labor Organization (ILO) found that the informal economy in Ghana accounts for over 80% of the workforce and contributes to over 50% of the country's GDP (ILO, 2018). Similarly, a study by the Nigerian National Bureau of Statistics found that the informal economy in Nigeria accounts for over 60% of the workforce and contributes to over 40% of the country's GDP (NBS, 2020).

However, other scholars have raised concerns about the negative impacts of the informal economy on economic growth and development. For example, a study by the World Bank found that the informal economy in Ghana and Nigeria is characterized by low productivity, low wages, and poor working conditions, which can hinder economic growth and development (World Bank, 2019).

Policy implications:
The informal economy in Ghana and Nigeria has significant policy implications. Governments in both countries have implemented policies aimed at formalizing the informal economy, such as registration and regulation of informal businesses, provision of training and skills development, and access to credit and other financial services (Ghana Ministry of Trade and Industry, 2019; Nigerian Ministry of Industry, Trade and Investment, 2020).

However, these policies have been criticized for being inadequate and ineffective. For example, a study by the African Center for Economic Transformation found that the registration and regulation of informal businesses in Ghana has been slow and uneven, with many businesses remaining unregistered and unregulated (ACET, 2020). Similarly, a study by the Nigerian Institute of Social and Economic Research found that the provision of training and skills development for informal workers in Nigeria has been limited and ineffective (NISER, 2020).

Conclusion:
The informal economy in Ghana and Nigeria is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon that requires a nuanced and multidisciplinary approach. Traditional knowledge systems, such as apprenticeship and informal entrepreneurship, play a significant role in the informal economy, while academic perspectives highlight the importance of the informal economy in promoting economic growth and development. However, the informal economy also raises concerns about exploitation, poverty, and lack of social protection. Governments in both countries need to implement effective policies aimed at formalizing the informal economy, providing training and skills development, and promoting economic growth and development.
